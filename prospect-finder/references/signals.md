# Signals — the thing that makes a person worth writing to

An observable is **one specific, recent, real fact** about this person or their
company that makes contacting them *now* make sense.

Not "you're growing" — "you hired twelve support reps last quarter".
Not "I love your content" — "your post about churn maths on the 12th".

The mindset shift, and it's the whole skill:

> **Bad research:** find their role and company size so I can fill the template.
> **Good research:** what is happening in their world *right now* that makes this
> relevant?

*(That framing, the tiering below and the freshness table are adapted from the
gtm-agents cold-email research playbook — see `../CREDITS.md`.)*

## Three properties, all required

1. **Specific** — a number, a name, a date, a quote. If it could be said about
   anyone else on the list, it isn't one
2. **Recent** — see the freshness table below
3. **Aligned** — it points at the problem you solve. A fascinating fact that has
   nothing to do with your product is a distraction, not an opener

## Budget: about a minute per person

Longer than that and the list is the wrong list. Work down these tiers and stop
at the first thing that clears the "so what?" test:

### Tier 1 — what they published (fastest, best hit rate)

A post, a talk, a changelog, a launch, a video, a comment thread they started.
It's public, it's dated, and it proves you actually looked.

### Tier 2 — what they're doing (the differentiator)

- **Hiring** — open roles say more about priorities than any about-page
- **Shipping** — a release, a redesign, a new pricing page
- **Funding, launches, moves** — new market, new office, new title
- **Public complaints** — reviews, support threads, a "does anyone know how to…"
- **Competitor activity** — they just adopted, or just left, something adjacent

### Tier 3 — what's on the profile (backup)

Role, tenure, stack, location, company size. Weakest tier: it's true of hundreds
of people and everyone can tell it came from a filter. Use it only as *context*
around a tier 1 or 2 signal, never as the opener itself.

## Freshness — signals expire

| Signal | Still usable for |
|---|---|
| A post or comment | ~30 days |
| Hiring activity | ~60 days |
| Funding / launch news | ~90 days |
| A public review or complaint | ~90 days (longer if unresolved) |
| Role or company change | ~90 days |
| Profile facts (stack, size, tenure) | no expiry, but no power either |

Record `observed_at` as **when the thing happened**, not when you found it. A
list that sits for six weeks before anyone writes to it is carrying expired
signals, and referencing a four-month-old post reads worse than not personalising
at all.

## The "so what?" test

Before writing a signal into a row, finish this sentence:

> *"They did X, **which means** they're probably dealing with Y."*

If the "which means" doesn't land — if the honest answer is "no idea, it was just
the most recent thing on their feed" — it's not a signal. Keep looking or drop
the person.

Examples that pass:

- *Posted three times about manual reporting → they're spending real hours on it*
- *Hiring two more ops people → the process is the bottleneck, not the headcount*
- *Left a 2-star review of a competitor citing onboarding → actively unhappy, and
  specifically about the thing you do differently*

Examples that fail:

- *Their company has 40 employees* — so what?
- *They liked a post about AI* — so did everyone
- *They've been in role 3 years* — genuinely, so what?

## When there's genuinely nothing

That's a signal too: **they're the wrong target**. Not a reason to write filler.

If it keeps happening across a source, the problem is upstream — the description
is too broad, or the platform doesn't expose what these people do. Go back to
step 1 rather than lowering the bar, because a list of unopenable rows costs more
than an empty one: someone will eventually mail it.
